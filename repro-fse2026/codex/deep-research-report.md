# Novelty Assessment of Cost-Aware Sampling for Performance Prediction of Configurable Software Systems

## Bottom line

Across the configurable-systems literature I could verify, the **dominant practice is to operationalize “sampling cost” as measurement count or sample size**, sometimes combined with prediction error, rather than as the **observed sum of per-configuration execution or benchmarking times**. The strongest and closest threat is indeed **Sarkar et al., ASE 2015**, but that paper’s cost model explicitly simplifies training and testing acquisition cost to **`2n`** under a 50:50 train/test split, which is a count-based abstraction, not an empirical time-based model. I did **not** find a configurable-systems paper that empirically tests whether sample size is a reliable proxy for actual measurement time, nor one that compares strategy rankings under **size-based** versus **empirical time-based** cost models. On that basis, **RQ1–RQ3 look genuinely novel**. **RQ4 is more nuanced**: the broader idea that conclusions vary across inputs, workloads, and performance properties is **not novel anymore**, but the **cost-aware** version of that question still appears open. citeturn7view0turn9view0turn35view0turn40view0turn42view0turn39search14

A careful one-sentence novelty claim would therefore be: **prior work on configurable-system sampling has mostly optimized or reported size-based effort, while your work tests whether this proxy is valid at all and whether empirical time-based cost changes the scientific conclusions.** citeturn7view0turn29view0turn18view0turn35view0

## What prior work actually models as cost

The key paper to pin down is **Sarkar et al., ASE 2015**. The paper defines a total cost that includes training-set measurement, testing-set measurement, model-building cost, and prediction-error cost, then simplifies that model by ignoring CART model-building cost and assuming equal-sized training and testing sets. The resulting formula is **`TotalCost(n) = 2n + ε_n · |S| · R`**, where `2n` is just the number of configurations in the training and testing sets. This is exactly the count-based abstraction you suspected: it treats each measured configuration as having unit acquisition cost and folds train+test acquisition into a single linear term. citeturn7view0turn9view0

That is not an isolated pattern. **Guo et al., ASE 2013** motivate the problem by saying measurement can be expensive, but evaluate feasibility with random samples of size **`N, 2N, 3N, M`**, explicitly justifying linear-in-features sample sizes as practically feasible because measurement is costly. Their effort proxy is sample size; the “time cost” experiment in that paper concerns the learning procedure’s runtime, not the empirical benchmark time needed to acquire labels. citeturn22view0turn23view0turn23view2turn23view3

Likewise, **Westermann et al., ASE 2012** define efficiency in terms of the **required number of measurement points** needed to reach an accurate model. Their iterative framework stops when relative prediction error on validation falls below a threshold, and they compare combinations of measurement-point selection, validation, and inference methods mainly by **number of measurements** plus MRE. The paper explicitly frames efficiency as “least possible number of measurements,” even while noting that the analyst may choose iteration sizes depending on the time to obtain one measurement and the time to analyze it. citeturn26view0

**Siegmund et al., FSE 2015** come close to your framing, because they explicitly seek Pareto-optimal tradeoffs between **measurement effort** and **prediction accuracy**. But their operationalization of measurement effort is still count-based: option-wise sampling is linear in the number of binary options, pair-wise is quadratic, and the figures/tables compare mean error against **number of measurements**. Their conclusion that “more configurations do not necessarily lead to more accurate predictions” is important, but it is still about **which configurations** matter for accuracy when effort is measured by counts, not about actual heterogeneity in benchmark time. citeturn29view0turn29view1turn29view2

A systematic review by **Pereira et al.** reinforces this reading. It explicitly notes that several works use a **sampling cost** notion, but in those studies the cost is typically “the total number of measurements required to train the model.” In other words, even the literature that talks about “cost” usually means **cardinality of measured samples**, not observed acquisition time. citeturn35view0

The one meaningful partial exception I found is **Siegmund et al., ICSE 2012**. That paper reports, for each heuristic, not only the number of measurements but also the **time in hours** needed for those measurements. So it does contain an empirical acquisition-effort measure. However, it does **not** use that to test whether equal-cost-per-configuration holds, does **not** control for equal `n` across strategies to compare summed measurement times, and does **not** compare rankings under a count-based cost model versus a time-based one. The time column is descriptive effort reporting, not an empirical proxy-validity study. citeturn25view0

## Direct answers to the search questions

On the first question—**which papers explicitly model or measure sampling cost, measurement effort, acquisition cost, labeling cost, or benchmarking cost**—the clearest configurable-systems papers are **Sarkar et al. 2015** on cost-efficient sampling; **Siegmund et al. 2012** on heuristic effort and measurement hours; **Siegmund et al. 2015** on measurement-effort versus prediction-accuracy tradeoffs; and **Westermann et al. 2012** on required measurement points to derive accurate models. Outside configurable systems but directly upstream conceptually are **Weiss and Tian 2008**, which treat classifier utility under data-acquisition and modeling costs, and **Last 2009**, which frames projective sampling in terms of acquisition/pre-processing cost, CPU cost, and error cost. citeturn7view0turn25view0turn29view0turn26view0turn43search8turn43search1

On the second question—**which papers assume cost proportional to sample size, and which measure actual per-configuration time/cost**—the literature is overwhelmingly in the first camp. **ASE 2015** explicitly reduces train+test acquisition to `2n`; **ASE 2013**, **FSE 2015**, **Fourier Learning 2015**, **WHAT/Spectral Learning**, **distance-based sampling**, and many later model-construction papers reason in terms of sample size, number of measurements, or testing effort reduction. The paper I found that most explicitly reports empirical acquisition time is **ICSE 2012**, but again as descriptive effort, not as the optimized cost model. citeturn7view0turn22view0turn29view2turn27view0turn31view0turn18view0turn25view0

On the third question—**has any paper empirically tested whether `n` or `n+m` is a reliable proxy for measured time**—I did **not** find one in the configurable-systems performance-prediction literature I could verify. The papers repeatedly say measurement is costly, and a few report hours or CPU years, but I found no controlled study correlating sample size with empirical total benchmark time or asking whether an `x%` reduction in `n` yields an `x%` reduction in acquisition time. citeturn25view0turn18view0turn35view0

On the fourth question—**has any paper compared strategy rankings under size-based cost versus empirical time-based cost**—I again found **no direct evidence**. Existing “cost-aware” comparisons in configurable systems are comparisons of **error versus count-based effort**. The closest candidate, **ASE 2015**, still ranks strategies under a cost model whose measurement term is `2n`, not empirical measured time. citeturn9view0turn29view0turn35view0

On the fifth question—**has any paper separated training sampling cost from evaluation/test-set cost, rather than adding both into the sampling objective**—the answer is subtle. Many papers **separate training and testing procedurally** for model evaluation, but they do not make that separation the core object of cost analysis. **ASE 2015** explicitly **adds both training and testing acquisition costs** into its objective and then collapses them into `2n`. By contrast, papers such as **Guo et al. 2013** and **Westermann et al. 2012** use train/test or train/validation splits operationally, but do not compare training-only versus training+test cost objectives. I did not find a paper whose contribution is precisely that separation. citeturn7view0turn22view0turn26view0

On the sixth question—**has any paper studied sensitivity across inputs and performance properties**—the answer is **yes, but mostly not in a cost-aware way**. **Pereira et al., ICPE 2020** show that on x264, the relative merits of sampling strategies vary across **17 videos** and **two properties**. **Lesoil et al., JSS 2023** conduct a large input-sensitivity study over **eight configurable systems** and explicitly question the usefulness of models trained on only one input. **Mühlbauer et al., ICSE 2023** show that single-workload approaches often do not generalize across workload variation and that workloads interact with configuration options in non-monotonous ways. **Lesoil et al., JSS 2024** push this further with input-aware prediction models and claim the first domain-agnostic empirical evaluation of ML methods for input-aware performance prediction. ## Assessment by research question

**RQ1 — Equal-cost-per-configuration assumption.** I did not find a prior configurable-systems paper that **tests this assumption directly** by holding training sample size fixed and comparing the **actual summed benchmark time** induced by different sampling strategies. The closest literature either assumes unit cost per measured configuration outright, as in **ASE 2015**, or reports acquisition time descriptively without framing it as a proxy-validity question, as in **ICSE 2012**. That makes RQ1 look **novel**. citeturn7view0turn25view0

**RQ2 — Sample size as proxy for empirical cost.** I did not find a paper that computes or studies the correlation between `n` and `Σ measurementTime(c)` in configurable software systems, nor one that asks whether reducing `n` by some fraction approximately preserves time reduction. Prior work consistently treats effort as count-based, even when it acknowledges that performance measurements are expensive or when it reports large total CPU budgets. RQ2 therefore looks **novel and unusually sharp**. citeturn18view0turn35view0turn7view0

**RQ3 — Impact of the cost model on conclusions.** This is the strongest novelty point. I found no configurable-systems paper that re-ranks sampling or sampling+learning strategies under **size-based** versus **empirical time-based** cost models, or that compares conclusions drawn from something like **MRE+sample-size** versus **MRE+measured-time**. Existing tradeoff studies compare error against number of measurements, not error against observed acquisition time. RQ3 appears **novel**. citeturn9view0turn29view0turn35view0

**RQ4 — Context sensitivity.** If this RQ is phrased simply as “do conclusions change across systems, workloads, inputs, and performance properties?”, then it is **not novel in general**; several recent papers already establish that broader point. If instead the claim is “do **cost-aware** conclusions based on empirical acquisition time remain stable across systems, inputs, and properties?”, then I did not find a paper answering that. So RQ4 is **partly novel only when tightly scoped to empirical time-based cost conclusions**. citeturn15search2turn40view0turn42view0turn39search14

## Closest threats to novelty

The closest threat to the paper’s **cost-aware** claim is still **Sarkar et al., ASE 2015**, because it is the most explicit configurable-systems paper on balancing prediction quality and acquisition effort. But the same paper is also the strongest support for your novelty hypothesis, because it reduces acquisition effort to the count term `2n` and therefore **builds the equal-cost-per-configuration assumption into the objective itself**. If your paper demonstrates that measured acquisition time is not well captured by `n`, you are not repeating ASE 2015; you are testing one of its central simplifications. citeturn7view0turn9view0

The second-closest threat is **Siegmund et al., ICSE 2012**, because it reports **actual measurement hours** in addition to measurement counts. If a reviewer seeks a “gotcha,” this is the paper they may cite. But its role is still partial: it reports empirical effort, yet it does not formulate or test the proxy-validity question you ask, does not compare strategies at fixed `n` for unequal time, and does not investigate ranking changes under time-based versus count-based cost models. citeturn25view0

For **RQ4**, the main threats are not cost papers at all, but the newer **input/workload sensitivity** papers. **Pereira et al. 2020**, **Lesoil et al. 2023**, **Mühlbauer et al. 2023**, and **Lesoil et al. 2024** collectively make it hard to claim novelty for “context matters” in the abstract. They do, however, leave open your sharper question: whether **empirical time-based cost conclusions** are stable across contexts. citeturn15search2turn40view0turn42view0turn39search14

The older data-mining papers **Weiss and Tian 2008** and **Last 2009** are conceptual precursors, not direct novelty killers. They show that utility-based sampling can include acquisition, modeling, and error costs, and that projective sampling can optimize expected utility under such costs. But they are not configurable-systems papers and do not study configuration-dependent measurement-time heterogeneity. They matter mostly because **ASE 2015 imports this utility framing into configurable software**. citeturn43search8turn43search1turn7view0

## Related-work paragraph you can reuse

Prior work on performance prediction for configurable software systems has long recognized that measuring configurations is expensive, but most studies operationalize this effort using **sample size** or **number of measurements** rather than the **observed execution time of the sampled configurations**. Guo et al. build predictors from random samples of size `N`, `2N`, `3N`, and `M`, treating feasible effort essentially as sample cardinality. Siegmund et al. and later performance-influence-model work similarly analyze tradeoffs between prediction accuracy and the number of measurements required. Sarkar et al. go one step further by introducing a composite “sampling cost,” but their configurable-systems instantiation simplifies acquisition cost to `2n` for equal-sized training and testing sets, thereby assuming uniform per-configuration measurement cost. More recent work has shown that conclusions about sampling and prediction can vary strongly across workloads, inputs, and performance properties, for example on x264 and other configurable systems, yet these studies do not revisit the underlying **size-as-cost** abstraction itself. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has systematically tested whether sample size is a reliable proxy for empirical benchmarking time, nor whether replacing count-based effort with measured time changes the ranking of sampling and learning strategies. citeturn22view0turn25view0turn29view0turn7view0turn15search2turn40view0turn42view0

## Papers that may invalidate or weaken the novelty claim

I did **not** find a paper that clearly invalidates the **full** novelty claim for **RQ1–RQ3**. The best counterexamples only weaken parts of it. **ASE 2015** weakens any claim that “nobody studied cost-aware sampling,” because it clearly did; but it simultaneously supports the more precise claim that prior configurable-systems work used a **size-based acquisition-cost abstraction**. **ICSE 2012** weakens any claim that “nobody ever reported empirical measurement effort,” because it did report hours; yet it stops short of studying the validity of `n` as a proxy or the effect of changing cost models on rankings. For **RQ4**, the input/workload papers meaningfully weaken any claim that context sensitivity itself is novel. citeturn7view0turn25view0turn40view0turn42view0

So the safest defensible novelty statement is: **existing literature either optimizes sample-count-based effort or studies input/workload sensitivity, but does not empirically test the sample-size proxy against measured acquisition time or compare strategic conclusions under count-based versus empirical time-based cost models.** citeturn7view0turn25view0turn35view0turn40view0
